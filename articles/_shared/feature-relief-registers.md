# Registers in architectural relief

## Definition and Grounded Significance

Course reading: Horizontal bands divide the Hampi royal relief programmes into successive fields of animals, people and courtly activity. Repetition helps organize a viewer’s movement along the monument. Each band must still be read for its own subject and preservation.

## Slide Context

**M1:1**

Course reading: The cover pairs a floral ceramic dish and floral fabric with a stone trader-and-horses relief under Ebb and Flow: Art and Globalism of Oceanic Networks. Repeated flowers connect ceramic and textile; the relief contributes the people and traded animals. This cross-media grouping argues for the circulation of goods, designs and artisanal knowledge through contact. The Paigah Tombs and Coromandel kalamkari hanging on slides 2–3 extend that argument into architectural ornament, textile making and human interaction.

**M1:7**

Course reading: The Krishna Temple hall follows the Hampi maps and precedes the Badami–Vijayanagara sculpture comparison. Its carved posts, horizontal beams and animal supports connect the scale of a building to the sculptural forms through which the next slide compares periods.

**M1:9, M1:10, M1:11**

Course reading: The location map, three-tiered platform and close views of carved bands develop one argument about the Mahanabami Dibba. Royal ceremony is materialized through spatial elevation and repeated scenes of animals and people. Narasimha and Chenchu Lakshmi keep religious imagery within that programme. The next tank and Rama Temple comparison asks how water, festival and monumental narrative relate.

**M1:12**

Course reading: The stepped tank is paired with the Rama Temple relief of festival and warfare. The juxtaposition asks how water architecture, movement and ritual imagery might relate to Narasimha and Vasantotsava. This is the lecture’s posed connection, while the preceding royal platform supplies the political and ceremonial setting.

**M1:13, M1:14**

Course reading: Platform friezes are compared with hero-stone imagery, followed by a pairing of hero and sati memorials. Registers, active bodies and commemorative scenes connect royal carving to other narrative traditions. The comparison does not collapse the distinct memorial subjects into one event or identity.

**M1:15**

Course reading: The early granite carving and later green-chlorite facing are placed together as phases of the Mahanabami Dibba. Differences in surface, cutting and figural handling are read historically within one monument. The preceding memorial comparison supplies a stylistic reference; the following horse-trade slide shifts attention to the people and goods represented.

**M1:16**

Course reading: The trader with horses is placed beside a wootz dagger under Trade connections, with textiles named in the lecture discussion. The grouping connects traded animals, metal and cloth to royal imagery. The collar and performer comparisons on slides 17–18 extend commerce into the movement of dress and visual forms.

**M1:17, M1:18**

Course reading: The cloud-collar figure, carved musicians or dancers, and Golconda coverlet form a sequence comparing costume and performance across image, stone and cloth. The queried Chinese identification is part of that comparison. Under the trade discussion, recurring forms become evidence for asking how people and designs move; the specific performers’ nationality remains an attribution question.

**M1:19**

Course reading: The Virupaksha procession mural is placed beside its temple gateway under Images of procession cross Media. Painted movement and monumental setting are read together, continuing the earlier relief and textile comparisons. The following royal bronzes and Lepakshi mural extend the question from processions to the representation of rulers.

**M1:21**

Course reading: Vijaya Vitthala friezes, animal-bearing columns and three warrior panels extend the enquiry into interregional imagery beyond the royal platform. Compare clothing, weapons, pose and composite animals within their architectural positions. Differences among the three warriors matter: they are separate carved figures, not duplicate views of one person.

**M1:25**

Course reading: The royal platform, horse trader, hero stone and warrior recap the module’s cultural/trade argument. Ceremony, commerce, commemoration and martial imagery contribute different evidence. Their grouping joins social and political functions to travelling forms; it does not reduce Hampi’s art to one exclusively religious or decorative meaning.

**M2:12**

Course reading: The Golconda coverlet’s pavilion, figures, animals and plants are followed by the Ramayana textile’s framed narrative. This sequence trains close looking before comparing courtly and devotional uses of the same broad textile practice. Shared technique leaves room for different subjects and ways of organizing a field.

## Distinctions

**Exam distractor:** “Every figure across a continuous relief band participates in one simultaneous event.”

**Why it is wrong:** A continuous band need not be one continuous narrative event, and figures in different registers need not be contemporaneous.
