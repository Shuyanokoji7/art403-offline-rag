# Craft agency and the distribution of credit

## Definition and Grounded Significance

Course reading: Print production, dye work and botanical illustration involve distinct skilled roles. The source records identify collaborative makers or Indian artists associated with named commissioners. The course’s social dimension asks whose labour becomes visible and whose name is retained in conventional attribution.

## Slide Context

**M1:1**

Course reading: The cover pairs a floral ceramic dish and floral fabric with a stone trader-and-horses relief under Ebb and Flow: Art and Globalism of Oceanic Networks. Repeated flowers connect ceramic and textile; the relief contributes the people and traded animals. This cross-media grouping argues for the circulation of goods, designs and artisanal knowledge through contact. The Paigah Tombs and Coromandel kalamkari hanging on slides 2–3 extend that argument into architectural ornament, textile making and human interaction.

**M1:16**

Course reading: The trader with horses is placed beside a wootz dagger under Trade connections, with textiles named in the lecture discussion. The grouping connects traded animals, metal and cloth to royal imagery. The collar and performer comparisons on slides 17–18 extend commerce into the movement of dress and visual forms.

**M1:25**

Course reading: The royal platform, horse trader, hero stone and warrior recap the module’s cultural/trade argument. Ceremony, commerce, commemoration and martial imagery contribute different evidence. Their grouping joins social and political functions to travelling forms; it does not reduce Hampi’s art to one exclusively religious or decorative meaning.

**M2:4, M2:5**

Course reading: The Srikalahasti drawing demonstration and Polavaram block-printing demonstration are paired across adjacent slides, with a finished Coromandel hanging beside the latter. They separate making with a pen, repeating with a block and the resulting coloured cloth. The comparison gives craft labour a place alongside design and trade.

**M3:8**

Course reading: The turbaned miniature detail introduces close attention to painted faces and clothing; the next slide locates Farrukh Beg within a travelling artist’s career and album culture. Stylistic observation and a named biography contribute different evidence to the discussion of moving artists.

**M3:13**

Course reading: The marbling demonstration is placed with Ascetic Riding a Nag, followed by another thin rider on a marbled horse. Process and finished imagery explain how abri patterns become part of a figure-ground composition. The technique comparison is firmer than assigning one exclusive spiritual or literary identity to each rider.

**M3:25**

Course reading: Farrukh Beg’s presumed self-portrait, Ascetic Riding a Nag and Sultan Ali Adil Shah I recap the module through travelling artists, marbled technique and courtly identity. Each supplies a different mechanism of connection. Their grouping makes movement of people, processes and imagery jointly significant.

**M4:1, M4:2, M4:3**

Course reading: Vosmaer’s flowers and Vermeer’s Geographer place an oceanic argument within a vase, textile, map and globe. The still life and inhabited interior connect collected goods with geographical knowledge and pictorial skill. The subsequent VOC material makes the commercial network explicit.

**M4:4, M4:5**

Course reading: The VOC ship model and lavish lobster-and-fruit still life connect transport with the consumption and display of goods. Between Amsterdam and Asia develops into a discussion of materiality and trade. The painted table is therefore read through economic circulation as well as arrangement, colour and texture.

**M4:6**

Course reading: The goldsmith’s balance, goods, customers and convex mirror follow the discussion of traded possessions. The shop makes exchange and skilled work visible within a densely organized interior. The next timepiece still life extends attention to crafted objects, time and the values attached to possession.

**M4:7, M4:8**

Course reading: The timepiece and armour still lifes group crafted objects with carefully rendered surfaces. Under the time devoted to making objects discussion, they connect labour and visual attention to possession, transience or status. Different selected objects support different meanings within the still-life format.

**M4:9, M4:10**

Course reading: Peacocks in a garden and dead partridges in a still life compare living display with arranged game. The trade-goods and exotic discussion asks how birds enter collections, consumption and images. Their shared animal subject does not make the scenes interchangeable or give them one fixed symbolic code.

**M5:3**

Course reading: The revived Dhaka muslin sari and transparency demonstration compare a worn textile with a close presentation of its fineness. Under Prized Trade Goods, cloth’s material quality becomes part of its value and circulation. The next Akbar image links such attention to clothing with political self-presentation.

**M5:14**

Course reading: The two Camel Fight images by or associated with Bihzad and Abd al-Samad are paired under Lineage or Silsila. A recurring subject and composition make transmission and emulation visible. The succeeding European-source studies extend this enquiry beyond one Persianate line of artistic association.

**M6:1**

Course reading: The Calcutta Art Studio Buddha and the Agra print introduce two uses of reproducible imagery: devotional circulation and a published architectural view. The Agra credits separate sketch, drawing and engraving. European print technology and South Asian subjects therefore require separate statements about medium, audience and labour.

**M6:3**

Course reading: The unfinished Pedana woodblocks and finished Polavaram cotton connect a carved matrix to textile printing. This precedes the European Saint Sebastian block-and-impression pair. The sequence compares a printing principle across supports and uses, while keeping the specific practices distinct.

**M6:7**

Course reading: The Sicri Gully and Salsette temple prints are followed by the Antiquities of India title page. Views, maker credits and antiquarian publication framing connect Indian sites with a British audience. Pictorial composition and the date of a published plate remain separate from the date of a visit or monument.

**M6:9, M6:12**

Course reading: Solvyns’s potters and bazaar, the Kanaree cave view and Roxburgh’s botanical plate form a sequence of people, places and plants under colonial documentation. Each image organizes knowledge differently. The grouping asks who observes, draws, commissions and publishes, as well as what is represented.

**M6:13, M6:14**

Course reading: The broom scene and cat-with-lobster Kalighat painting are followed by the cat motif in a Battala print. Social commentary and a recognizable subject pass between painting and print. The repeated cat is therefore a comparison of pictorial treatment and circulation, not evidence that the two objects share one manufacturing process.

**M6:17**

Course reading: The lithographed book cover, tiger-shaped script and hawk-shaped script compare writing as readable content and constructed image. Lithography and colour lithography provide different printed realizations. The grouping connects text, animal outline and circulation rather than treating the animal forms as natural-history studies.

**M6:18**

Course reading: The Bombay scene and Annoda Prosad Bagchi’s Vijaya pair different makers and subjects within the print economy. They follow the book and calligraphic prints and precede studio devotional sheets. The comparison expands the field of print production beyond a single colonial publisher or one religious audience.

**M6:19, M6:20, M6:21**

Course reading: Kali/Tara, the Calcutta Art Studio Buddha and the Pune Mahavir print form a sequence of studio devotional images. Different religious subjects use reproducible colour imagery and commercial publication. The significant distinction is between the regional subject, the technology and the agency of the producing studio.

**M8:7**

Course reading: The Mumbai trade-textile graphic, ROM display and Southeast Asian Ramayana textile connect contemporary curatorial framing with different uses of Indian cloth. Their sequence distinguishes an exhibition identity, an arranged group of garments and an individual ceremonial textile. Market, subject and present display are different parts of its social history.

## Distinctions

**Exam distractor:** “The named designer or commissioner must have completed every physical stage of manufacture.”

**Why it is wrong:** A commissioner funds or requests an object, a designer organizes it, and artisans carry out material operations. The textile demonstrations and workshop examples make those distinct contributions visible.
