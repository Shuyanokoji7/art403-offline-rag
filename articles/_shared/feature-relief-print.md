# Relief printing

## Definition and Grounded Significance

Course reading: In relief printing the inked raised surface prints while cut-away areas remain unprinted. Woodblock prints are a central example. The image is multiplied through a matrix, unlike a carved architectural relief that remains on its original support.

## Slide Context

**M6:3**

Course reading: The unfinished Pedana woodblocks and finished Polavaram cotton connect a carved matrix to textile printing. This precedes the European Saint Sebastian block-and-impression pair. The sequence compares a printing principle across supports and uses, while keeping the specific practices distinct.

**M6:4**

Course reading: The Saint Sebastian woodblock and its impression are paired to make relief printing legible as a transfer process. Raised and cut-away areas relate to printed and unprinted areas. The following lithographic demonstration shows a different printing principle, so all reproducible images cannot be grouped as woodcuts.

**M6:5, M6:6**

Course reading: The lithographic stone demonstration and relief-printing video compare planographic and raised-surface printing. Matrix, inking and transfer provide the distinctions. The subsequent landscape prints show how reproducible processes serve published views, rather than making process and subject the same category.

**M6:7**

Course reading: The Sicri Gully and Salsette temple prints are followed by the Antiquities of India title page. Views, maker credits and antiquarian publication framing connect Indian sites with a British audience. Pictorial composition and the date of a published plate remain separate from the date of a visit or monument.

**M6:14**

Course reading: The broom scene and cat-with-lobster Kalighat painting are followed by the cat motif in a Battala print. Social commentary and a recognizable subject pass between painting and print. The repeated cat is therefore a comparison of pictorial treatment and circulation, not evidence that the two objects share one manufacturing process.

**M6:15, M6:16**

Course reading: Shiva playing a veena, the two Mahant–Elokeshi scenes and Durga with her family show the range of indigenous popular imagery. Religious subjects and a public social narrative share the market without becoming one story. Distinguish the separate encounter and violence scenes when reading the paired scandal images.

**M6:18**

Course reading: The Bombay scene and Annoda Prosad Bagchi’s Vijaya pair different makers and subjects within the print economy. They follow the book and calligraphic prints and precede studio devotional sheets. The comparison expands the field of print production beyond a single colonial publisher or one religious audience.

**M7:1, M7:3**

Course reading: The Great Wave, the introductory ukiyo-e image and Lake Chuzenji establish a Japanese print vocabulary of framing, flat colour, landscape and scale. They prepare the following Van Gogh–Eisen comparison. The Japanese examples remain distinct works rather than interchangeable illustrations of one national style.

**M7:10**

Course reading: La Japonaise, Cassatt’s The Letter and Mizuno’s After the Bath compare costume performance, domestic pictorial design and a Japanese print. The three illustrate different forms of Japanese reception. Cassatt’s intaglio medium remains distinct from woodblock, and Mizuno’s later print cannot be her direct source.

**M7:11**

Course reading: Hokusai’s Mannen Bridge and Monet’s Japanese bridge are paired through the bridge’s graphic curve. The populated urban crossing and garden pond have different scales, settings and media. The comparison makes a formal relation explicit without establishing that Monet copied this exact impression.

## Distinctions

**Exam distractor:** “A sculptural relief and a woodblock impression are the same kind of object.”

**Why it is wrong:** A sculptural relief is the carved object seen directly. A woodblock’s raised inked surface is a matrix used to transfer an image onto another support; the resulting impression is the print.
