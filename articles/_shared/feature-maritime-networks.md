# Maritime networks: goods, people and knowledge

## Definition and Grounded Significance

Course reading: The Belitung cargo and Indian textile histories provide material cases of long-distance circulation. The course extends this inquiry to makers, images and cultural practices. Connections need object-specific evidence. Broad oceanic framing does not prove a complete itinerary for every artwork.

## Slide Context

**M1:1**

Course reading: The cover pairs a floral ceramic dish and floral fabric with a stone trader-and-horses relief under Ebb and Flow: Art and Globalism of Oceanic Networks. Repeated flowers connect ceramic and textile; the relief contributes the people and traded animals. This cross-media grouping argues for the circulation of goods, designs and artisanal knowledge through contact. The Paigah Tombs and Coromandel kalamkari hanging on slides 2–3 extend that argument into architectural ornament, textile making and human interaction.

**M1:16**

Course reading: The trader with horses is placed beside a wootz dagger under Trade connections, with textiles named in the lecture discussion. The grouping connects traded animals, metal and cloth to royal imagery. The collar and performer comparisons on slides 17–18 extend commerce into the movement of dress and visual forms.

**M1:25**

Course reading: The royal platform, horse trader, hero stone and warrior recap the module’s cultural/trade argument. Ceremony, commerce, commemoration and martial imagery contribute different evidence. Their grouping joins social and political functions to travelling forms; it does not reduce Hampi’s art to one exclusively religious or decorative meaning.

**M4:1, M4:2, M4:3**

Course reading: Vosmaer’s flowers and Vermeer’s Geographer place an oceanic argument within a vase, textile, map and globe. The still life and inhabited interior connect collected goods with geographical knowledge and pictorial skill. The subsequent VOC material makes the commercial network explicit.

**M4:4, M4:5**

Course reading: The VOC ship model and lavish lobster-and-fruit still life connect transport with the consumption and display of goods. Between Amsterdam and Asia develops into a discussion of materiality and trade. The painted table is therefore read through economic circulation as well as arrangement, colour and texture.

**M4:6**

Course reading: The goldsmith’s balance, goods, customers and convex mirror follow the discussion of traded possessions. The shop makes exchange and skilled work visible within a densely organized interior. The next timepiece still life extends attention to crafted objects, time and the values attached to possession.

**M4:7, M4:8**

Course reading: The timepiece and armour still lifes group crafted objects with carefully rendered surfaces. Under the time devoted to making objects discussion, they connect labour and visual attention to possession, transience or status. Different selected objects support different meanings within the still-life format.

**M4:9, M4:10**

Course reading: Peacocks in a garden and dead partridges in a still life compare living display with arranged game. The trade-goods and exotic discussion asks how birds enter collections, consumption and images. Their shared animal subject does not make the scenes interchangeable or give them one fixed symbolic code.

**M8:1, M8:2**

Course reading: The Ahmedabad exhibition poster frames its title with a floral trade-textile detail. The original cloth becomes the visual identity of a contemporary exhibition about distant markets. The ensuing museum sequence examines how present displays assemble evidence for those historical connections.

**M8:3**

Course reading: The Interwoven Globe entrance banner and gallery sightline connect an exhibition’s public framing with its arrangement of textiles and images. Position, scale and the view through the gallery invite cross-object comparisons. The next Asia in Amsterdam installation provides a second curatorial method for presenting historical circulation.

**M8:4**

Course reading: Asia in Amsterdam places imported Asian goods near paintings that represent luxury consumption, with vessels and furniture in cases and textiles or pictures on walls. The entrance image and successive gallery views make trade visible through juxtaposition. A painting of a vessel and an exhibited vessel have complementary evidential roles.

**M8:7**

Course reading: The Mumbai trade-textile graphic, ROM display and Southeast Asian Ramayana textile connect contemporary curatorial framing with different uses of Indian cloth. Their sequence distinguishes an exhibition identity, an arranged group of garments and an individual ceremonial textile. Market, subject and present display are different parts of its social history.

**M8:9, M8:10, M8:12**

Course reading: Belitung bowls arranged as waves around a dhow are compared with underwater documentation and Kerkar’s encrusted cross. The museum uses repeated cargo to give production and shipping a spatial form; the underwater image records another stage of the objects’ lives. The cross introduces the ocean’s material participation in contemporary making.

**M8:14**

Course reading: Kerkar’s cross and the Museum of Goa title card develop the question of collaborating with the ocean. Encrustation, the cross form and its documentation connect material change to a recognizable cultural sign. This moves beyond the Belitung display’s reconstruction of past shipping into present artistic agency.

**M8:16**

Course reading: The Fort Kochi opening poster and two It’s my Biennale portrait designs put an international exhibition into ordinary local streets. Repetition and the first-person slogan connect public ownership with place. The following installations show how the event’s regional identity is developed through particular works and historical connections.

**M8:17**

Course reading: Raghavan’s container-ship work connects the Biblical Tarshish reference with contemporary shipping under Biblical Ties and Trade. It follows the street campaign and precedes Tallur’s tile installation. The sequence makes historical narrative, current commerce and local material history different ways of reclaiming global ties.

**M8:18**

Course reading: Tallur’s seated figure with a log is paired with Veni, vidi, vici’s passage between banks of roof tiles. Bodily encounter and material transformation connect the works, while their arrangements remain distinct. The Mangalore tile heading brings mission-industry history into the contemporary installation.

**M8:19**

Course reading: The framed elephant image is placed in relation to Quintessential’s screen, sculptural objects and fragmented forms. Under the Elephanta and colonial-legacy heading, archival representation and contemporary installation rework historical authority. Precise spatial relationships supply the curatorial argument, not the mere fact that several objects share a room.

**M8:20**

Course reading: The Vijaya Vitthala warrior, Ascetic Riding a Nag and Père Tanguy form the final recap. Stone carving, a marbled painting and a European portrait surrounded by Japanese prints supply three different histories of connection. Reclaiming oceanic ties means attending to those mechanisms of encounter and agency rather than treating regions as visually isolated.

## Distinctions

**Exam distractor:** “European companies created the Asian trade networks through which Indian textiles circulated.”

**Why it is wrong:** Course reading: Trade networks existed before European companies; their later participation does not make them the origin of all Asian exchange.
