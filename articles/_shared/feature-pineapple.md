# Pineapple motifs and changing contexts

## Definition and Grounded Significance

Course reading: MAP and the Museum of Christian Art compare a kalamkari palampore with a chasuble through the pineapple motif and emphasize changing meanings. Their published description supports the comparison, not a transcript-level claim about every detail. An exact plant identification in a slide still requires a resolved form.

## Slide Context

**M1:18**

Course reading: The cloud-collar figure, carved musicians or dancers, and Golconda coverlet form a sequence comparing costume and performance across image, stone and cloth. The queried Chinese identification is part of that comparison. Under the trade discussion, recurring forms become evidence for asking how people and designs move; the specific performers’ nationality remains an attribution question.

**M2:11**

Course reading: The Golconda coverlet’s pavilion, figures, animals and plants are followed by the Ramayana textile’s framed narrative. This sequence trains close looking before comparing courtly and devotional uses of the same broad textile practice. Shared technique leaves room for different subjects and ways of organizing a field.

**M2:13**

Course reading: The Golconda coverlet is paired with the hanging of Manikkavachar and Meenakshi’s wedding. Courtly imagery and religious narrative distinguish the two textiles even where borders and repeated figures invite formal comparison. The heading asks how to differentiate kalamkari works, not how to treat them as one subject.

**M2:14**

Course reading: A coverlet detail and the north doorway of Hayat Baksh’s tomb are paired under Pineapple: a fruit of the trade networks. The lecture follows a plant motif between textile and architecture to connect decoration with moving goods and designs. It extends the earlier comparisons without equating the objects’ functions.

## Distinctions

**Exam distractor:** “A shared pineapple means a palampore and a chasuble had identical functions.”

**Why it is wrong:** Course reading: A shared pineapple motif does not mean a trade textile and a liturgical garment had identical functions or symbolism.
