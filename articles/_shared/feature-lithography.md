# Lithography: planographic printing

## Definition and Grounded Significance

Course reading: Lithography prints from a prepared surface through differential acceptance of grease and water rather than raised blocks or engraved recesses. It enabled circulating images and text. The technique’s origin must be separated from the religious or regional subject it depicts.

## Slide Context

**M6:3**

Course reading: The unfinished Pedana woodblocks and finished Polavaram cotton connect a carved matrix to textile printing. This precedes the European Saint Sebastian block-and-impression pair. The sequence compares a printing principle across supports and uses, while keeping the specific practices distinct.

**M6:4**

Course reading: The Saint Sebastian woodblock and its impression are paired to make relief printing legible as a transfer process. Raised and cut-away areas relate to printed and unprinted areas. The following lithographic demonstration shows a different printing principle, so all reproducible images cannot be grouped as woodcuts.

**M6:5, M6:6**

Course reading: The lithographic stone demonstration and relief-printing video compare planographic and raised-surface printing. Matrix, inking and transfer provide the distinctions. The subsequent landscape prints show how reproducible processes serve published views, rather than making process and subject the same category.

**M6:7**

Course reading: The Sicri Gully and Salsette temple prints are followed by the Antiquities of India title page. Views, maker credits and antiquarian publication framing connect Indian sites with a British audience. Pictorial composition and the date of a published plate remain separate from the date of a visit or monument.

**M6:17**

Course reading: The lithographed book cover, tiger-shaped script and hawk-shaped script compare writing as readable content and constructed image. Lithography and colour lithography provide different printed realizations. The grouping connects text, animal outline and circulation rather than treating the animal forms as natural-history studies.

**M6:18**

Course reading: The Bombay scene and Annoda Prosad Bagchi’s Vijaya pair different makers and subjects within the print economy. They follow the book and calligraphic prints and precede studio devotional sheets. The comparison expands the field of print production beyond a single colonial publisher or one religious audience.

**M7:9**

Course reading: Jane Avril and Divan Japonais place Japanese-inspired cropping and contour within Parisian entertainment publicity. Performer, spectator and instrument are organized differently in the two posters. Their subject and lithographic process remain distinct from Japanese woodblock manufacture.

## Distinctions

**Exam distractor:** “A South Asian religious subject establishes a South Asian origin for lithographic technology.”

**Why it is wrong:** Course reading: A South Asian deity in a lithograph identifies the subject, not an indigenous origin for lithographic technology.
