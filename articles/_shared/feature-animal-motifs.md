# Animal motifs: identification before symbolism

## Definition and Grounded Significance

Course reading: Animals can function as narrative participants, compositional accents, attributes or decoration. Their presence in Islamic-world arts is well documented. It assigns no automatic political, moral or religious meaning to every animal.

## Slide Context

**M2:1**

Course reading: The full palampore and its detail introduce Coromandel textiles through overall field organization and close motif comparison. The production map on slide 2 then connects this object-centred reading to named places and routes, while the later demonstrations reveal the makers’ processes.

**M2:6, M2:7**

Course reading: The five-panel tent and enlarged two-headed bird are compared with a Manga malai necklace and a Dutch-cemetery gravestone. The Gandaberunda form connects textile, jewellery and carved stone across different uses. The grouping demonstrates motif mobility; the ritual, military and commemorative functions must still be distinguished.

**M2:9**

Course reading: The Bijapur mihrab and Golconda gateway extend the textile discussion into architectural framing and ornament. Compare arched compartments, vegetal design and surface pattern with the preceding hangings. The next Dutch warehouse introduces the commercial infrastructure through which textiles and designs circulated.

**M2:13**

Course reading: The Golconda coverlet is paired with the hanging of Manikkavachar and Meenakshi’s wedding. Courtly imagery and religious narrative distinguish the two textiles even where borders and repeated figures invite formal comparison. The heading asks how to differentiate kalamkari works, not how to treat them as one subject.

**M3:9**

Course reading: Ibrahim Adil Shah on Atash Khan is paired with enthroned Sarasvati under cultural amalgamation. Courtly self-presentation and a deity of learning and music share the discussion. The next Kitab-e-Nauras page and Sangeet Mahal translate this relationship into text, patronage and a musical setting.

**M3:18**

Course reading: The zoomorphic dagger hilt is followed by Sultan Ali Adil Shah I under a question about their relationship. A portable court object and a ruler’s portrait link animal ornament, costume and elite identity. The juxtaposition asks how object culture and represented kingship illuminate one another.

**M3:23**

Course reading: The Golconda Adoration of the Christ Child and Badshahi Ashurkhana interior share the heading Amalgamation of Persianate and European Themes. Painting and ritual architecture demonstrate different forms of religious and visual practice in the same regional discussion. They are complementary examples, not evidence that both serve one ritual.

**M3:24**

Course reading: Collaert’s bird engraving, the parrot-and-ram painting and the dervish gathering place animal imagery and human settings side by side. The sequence asks how motifs and ways of seeing move between European print and Deccani painting. A related bird or animal does not erase differences of subject, composition and use.

**M4:9, M4:10**

Course reading: Peacocks in a garden and dead partridges in a still life compare living display with arranged game. The trade-goods and exotic discussion asks how birds enter collections, consumption and images. Their shared animal subject does not make the scenes interchangeable or give them one fixed symbolic code.

**M5:2**

Course reading: The Mughal and Iranian animal carpets are placed within the question of continental and maritime trade. Their shared textile format supports comparison of animal imagery and production contexts across regions. The next muslin examples extend the enquiry from patterned furnishing to the material qualities of cloth.

**M5:5, M5:6**

Course reading: Basawan’s Virgin and Child is paired across adjacent slides with a work after Luini. The shared mother-and-child subject supports comparison of gesture, setting and modelling across Mughal and European images. The later date of the displayed Luini copy prevents treating that particular object as Basawan’s direct model.

**M5:13**

Course reading: The two Camel Fight images by or associated with Bihzad and Abd al-Samad are paired under Lineage or Silsila. A recurring subject and composition make transmission and emulation visible. The succeeding European-source studies extend this enquiry beyond one Persianate line of artistic association.

**M5:16**

Course reading: The queried Dürer Crucifixion, Abu’l Hasan’s Saint John and Abdullah Khan’s hawking scene place European print study alongside Mughal portraiture and courtly activity. These examples belong to the lineage discussion while differing in subject and making process. A European religious figure supplies a visual resource, not the identity of every local patron.

**M6:13**

Course reading: The broom scene and cat-with-lobster Kalighat painting are followed by the cat motif in a Battala print. Social commentary and a recognizable subject pass between painting and print. The repeated cat is therefore a comparison of pictorial treatment and circulation, not evidence that the two objects share one manufacturing process.

## Distinctions

**Exam distractor:** “The same animal has one unchanged symbolic meaning in every linked image.”

**Why it is wrong:** Course reading: An animal’s recurrence across different objects does not prove identical symbolism, shared patronage or a common workshop.
