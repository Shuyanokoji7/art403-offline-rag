# Japonisme: reception and transformation

## Definition and Grounded Significance

Course reading: Japonisme concerns European responses to Japanese art and objects. Artists adapted viewpoints, contours, cropping and decorative arrangements in different ways. Cassatt’s documented intaglio process and Monet’s oil painting show that influence need not mean importing the source medium unchanged.

## Slide Context

**M7:1, M7:2, M7:3**

Course reading: The Great Wave, the introductory ukiyo-e image and Lake Chuzenji establish a Japanese print vocabulary of framing, flat colour, landscape and scale. They prepare the following Van Gogh–Eisen comparison. The Japanese examples remain distinct works rather than interchangeable illustrations of one national style.

**M7:4**

Course reading: Eisen’s courtesan and Van Gogh’s painted version present a direct source-and-adaptation pair. Figure and costume are retained while palette, border imagery and medium change. The next Père Tanguy portrait extends appropriation into collecting and assembling Japanese images within a European painting.

**M7:5, M7:6**

Course reading: Père Tanguy’s Japanese-print backdrop is followed by Sunflowers and Almond Blossoms under Van Gogh’s imagined Japan. Collecting, colour, contour and cropped plant forms offer different ways to engage Japanese imagery. Japanese influence is therefore broader than a Japanese subject or the direct copy of one print.

**M7:7**

Course reading: Zola’s portrait and the enlarged reproductions on its wall make artistic association visible inside an interior. Olympia, a Japanese figure and a monochrome group are objects of pictorial reference within Manet’s painting. The components connect collecting to portrait identity without becoming separately inspected original prints.

**M7:8**

Course reading: The Art Institute Water Lilies and MoMA’s multi-panel work compare related subject matter at different scales. The gallery arrangement adds seating, open floor and viewing distance to the painted surface. Compare individual works and the bodily viewing conditions rather than treating every Water Lilies image as one object.

**M7:9**

Course reading: Jane Avril and Divan Japonais place Japanese-inspired cropping and contour within Parisian entertainment publicity. Performer, spectator and instrument are organized differently in the two posters. Their subject and lithographic process remain distinct from Japanese woodblock manufacture.

**M7:10**

Course reading: La Japonaise, Cassatt’s The Letter and Mizuno’s After the Bath compare costume performance, domestic pictorial design and a Japanese print. The three illustrate different forms of Japanese reception. Cassatt’s intaglio medium remains distinct from woodblock, and Mizuno’s later print cannot be her direct source.

**M7:11**

Course reading: Hokusai’s Mannen Bridge and Monet’s Japanese bridge are paired through the bridge’s graphic curve. The populated urban crossing and garden pond have different scales, settings and media. The comparison makes a formal relation explicit without establishing that Monet copied this exact impression.

**M7:13**

Course reading: The photographic portraits of Monet and Cassatt are followed by Beato’s staged performers. These photographs connect the artists and Japan to specific modes of representation. Sitter identification, photographic authorship, staging and hand-colouring are separate questions rather than one transparent documentary fact.

**M7:14**

Course reading: The Tokyo street photograph and Yoshikazu’s print of French people using a camera reverse the direction of looking. Japan appears as an urban photographic subject while Europeans become the subject of a Japanese print. Orientalism/Occidentalism is therefore discussed through representation and agency, not simply the nationality of an image’s subject.

**M8:20**

Course reading: The Vijaya Vitthala warrior, Ascetic Riding a Nag and Père Tanguy form the final recap. Stone carving, a marbled painting and a European portrait surrounded by Japanese prints supply three different histories of connection. Reclaiming oceanic ties means attending to those mechanisms of encounter and agency rather than treating regions as visually isolated.

## Distinctions

**Exam distractor:** “Japonisme requires Japanese subject matter and Japanese woodblock manufacture.”

**Why it is wrong:** Japonisme is not the same as Japanese authorship, Japanese subject matter or Japanese woodblock manufacture.
