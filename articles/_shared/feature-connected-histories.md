# Connected histories as the course method

## Definition and Grounded Significance

Course reading: The course reads art through movement among places, communities and media. Textile trade, Persianate court culture and imported print adaptation supply documented examples. The methodological claim is to examine evidenced relationships while retaining different regional histories. It is not a claim that all similarities share one cause.

## Slide Context

**M1:1, M1:2, M1:3, M1:4**

Course reading: The cover pairs a floral ceramic dish and floral fabric with a stone trader-and-horses relief under Ebb and Flow: Art and Globalism of Oceanic Networks. Repeated flowers connect ceramic and textile; the relief contributes the people and traded animals. This cross-media grouping argues for the circulation of goods, designs and artisanal knowledge through contact. The Paigah Tombs and Coromandel kalamkari hanging on slides 2–3 extend that argument into architectural ornament, textile making and human interaction.

**M1:5, M1:6**

Course reading: The empire and city maps establish several scales for reading the Mahanabami Dibba: territorial power, the Hampi landscape and individual monuments. The following temple hall and sculpture comparison turn this geographical frame into an investigation of structure, carving and historical difference.

**M2:2**

Course reading: The Coromandel map, route diagram and riverside temple landscape connect textile production to places, movement and built environments. This sequence leads from the opening palampore to named making practices. A historical map and a modern route diagram contribute different kinds of geographical evidence.

**M2:10**

Course reading: The Dutch warehouse follows the textile–architecture comparison and precedes the detailed reading of the Golconda coverlet. It places patterned goods and their imagery within trade infrastructure. The course asks how markets and contact participate in making visuals, as well as moving finished objects.

**M2:15**

Course reading: The Coromandel dodot moves the discussion from motif comparison to use in Southeast Asia. Its pairing in the sequence with European chintz garments on slide 16 shows a production region serving different dress forms and markets. Manufacture and destination are separate roles in the argument.

**M2:16**

Course reading: The chintz jacket and petticoat form a coordinated European dress ensemble. Following the Javanese dodot, they show Indian cotton entering distinct overseas clothing practices. Shared textile circulation therefore produces different social uses and silhouettes, rather than one uniform export style.

**M3:1, M3:2**

Course reading: The Persianate World / Persian Cosmopolis discussion uses Siesta to connect a Deccani scene with a wider courtly visual culture. The following madrasa comparison extends the enquiry from painting to architecture. Persianate connection is a framework for local adaptation, not a statement that every work was made in Iran.

**M3:4**

Course reading: Mahmud Gawan Madrasa is compared with Ulugh Beg’s madrasa and the Registan under Timurid Central Asian culture in Deccan. Monumental entrances, towers and patterned surfaces supply the architectural comparison. The next Bidar landscape slide broadens the enquiry to water, gardens and the siting of buildings.

**M3:6**

Course reading: The Bidri sailabchi and Iranian ghalamzani vessel compare decorative metalworking across Deccan and Iran. Contrasting inlay on a dark ground and worked surface detail direct attention to different processes. This object-making slide extends the architectural comparison into portable craft.

**M3:8**

Course reading: The turbaned miniature detail introduces close attention to painted faces and clothing; the next slide locates Farrukh Beg within a travelling artist’s career and album culture. Stylistic observation and a named biography contribute different evidence to the discussion of moving artists.

**M5:1**

Course reading: The Mughal and Iranian animal carpets are placed within the question of continental and maritime trade. Their shared textile format supports comparison of animal imagery and production contexts across regions. The next muslin examples extend the enquiry from patterned furnishing to the material qualities of cloth.

**M5:4**

Course reading: Akbar and Adham Khan is used to discuss sartorial choices and a ruler’s legitimacy after the muslin sequence. Clothing operates as a political visual language, not simply surface pattern. The following Christian-subject paintings extend the enquiry into the Mughal court’s selective use of different visual resources.

**M5:5**

Course reading: Basawan’s Virgin and Child is paired across adjacent slides with a work after Luini. The shared mother-and-child subject supports comparison of gesture, setting and modelling across Mughal and European images. The later date of the displayed Luini copy prevents treating that particular object as Basawan’s direct model.

**M5:7**

Course reading: Akbar’s combat-of-ascetics scene and Leonardo’s Virgin of the Rocks place Mughal atelier practice beside European Renaissance composition. Crowded action and the contained devotional grouping provide different organizations of figures and space. The sequence broadens artistic encounter beyond a claim of simple copying.

**M5:10**

Course reading: The Mughal Deposition follows the church and European painting sequence under Derivative? Altered? Creative? The familiar Christian subject becomes a test of adaptation in figures, setting and handling. Slide 17 returns to it beside Raimondi’s print so that a broad encounter can be examined as a particular composition comparison.

**M5:12**

Course reading: Ghulam’s Virgin and Child sits within the discussion of collecting and studying classical texts from diverse traditions at the Mughal court. The image becomes a case of active selection and translation between pictorial worlds. The following Bihzad–Abd al-Samad comparison makes artistic lineage a more specific mechanism.

**M5:18**

Course reading: The Gulshan sheet assembles a Luna engraving with Persian drawings of a seated man, a group of men and a lion. The whole and its components must be read together: collecting, cutting, mounting and framing create a new relationship among previously separate images. One page therefore need not have one maker, date or original medium.

**M5:19, M5:20**

Course reading: The full Jahangir–Malik Ambar allegory and its details connect the emperor’s body to a mapped globe, cosmic supports and a defeated enemy. The India-centred globe is part of a political claim. Repeated enlargement trains attention to the small devices through which cartographic form becomes imperial imagery.

**M5:21**

Course reading: Jahangir’s embrace of Shah Abbas uses a mapped globe and unequal figure scale for an allegory of sovereignty; the next folio places Jahangir with Jesus in separate images. The sequence compares the court’s political and religious visual resources. An embrace or shared page does not establish equal power or a single religious identity.

**M5:23**

Course reading: Shah Jahan’s halo, angels and portrait pendant are followed by a comparison with the standing-ruler drawing associated in the lecture with Rembrandt. The sequence tracks courtly self-representation and its reception across media. Maker, represented ruler and later interpreter perform different roles in that connection.

**M8:1, M8:2**

Course reading: The Ahmedabad exhibition poster frames its title with a floral trade-textile detail. The original cloth becomes the visual identity of a contemporary exhibition about distant markets. The ensuing museum sequence examines how present displays assemble evidence for those historical connections.

**M8:3**

Course reading: The Interwoven Globe entrance banner and gallery sightline connect an exhibition’s public framing with its arrangement of textiles and images. Position, scale and the view through the gallery invite cross-object comparisons. The next Asia in Amsterdam installation provides a second curatorial method for presenting historical circulation.

**M8:4**

Course reading: Asia in Amsterdam places imported Asian goods near paintings that represent luxury consumption, with vessels and furniture in cases and textiles or pictures on walls. The entrance image and successive gallery views make trade visible through juxtaposition. A painting of a vessel and an exhibited vessel have complementary evidential roles.

**M8:7**

Course reading: The Mumbai trade-textile graphic, ROM display and Southeast Asian Ramayana textile connect contemporary curatorial framing with different uses of Indian cloth. Their sequence distinguishes an exhibition identity, an arranged group of garments and an individual ceremonial textile. Market, subject and present display are different parts of its social history.

**M8:9, M8:10**

Course reading: Belitung bowls arranged as waves around a dhow are compared with underwater documentation and Kerkar’s encrusted cross. The museum uses repeated cargo to give production and shipping a spatial form; the underwater image records another stage of the objects’ lives. The cross introduces the ocean’s material participation in contemporary making.

**M8:14**

Course reading: Kerkar’s cross and the Museum of Goa title card develop the question of collaborating with the ocean. Encrustation, the cross form and its documentation connect material change to a recognizable cultural sign. This moves beyond the Belitung display’s reconstruction of past shipping into present artistic agency.

**M8:16**

Course reading: The Fort Kochi opening poster and two It’s my Biennale portrait designs put an international exhibition into ordinary local streets. Repetition and the first-person slogan connect public ownership with place. The following installations show how the event’s regional identity is developed through particular works and historical connections.

**M8:17**

Course reading: Raghavan’s container-ship work connects the Biblical Tarshish reference with contemporary shipping under Biblical Ties and Trade. It follows the street campaign and precedes Tallur’s tile installation. The sequence makes historical narrative, current commerce and local material history different ways of reclaiming global ties.

**M8:18**

Course reading: Tallur’s seated figure with a log is paired with Veni, vidi, vici’s passage between banks of roof tiles. Bodily encounter and material transformation connect the works, while their arrangements remain distinct. The Mangalore tile heading brings mission-industry history into the contemporary installation.

**M8:19**

Course reading: The framed elephant image is placed in relation to Quintessential’s screen, sculptural objects and fragmented forms. Under the Elephanta and colonial-legacy heading, archival representation and contemporary installation rework historical authority. Precise spatial relationships supply the curatorial argument, not the mere fact that several objects share a room.

**M8:20**

Course reading: The Vijaya Vitthala warrior, Ascetic Riding a Nag and Père Tanguy form the final recap. Stone carving, a marbled painting and a European portrait surrounded by Japanese prints supply three different histories of connection. Reclaiming oceanic ties means attending to those mechanisms of encounter and agency rather than treating regions as visually isolated.

## Distinctions

**Exam distractor:** “Connected histories means every similarity has one common origin and an uninterrupted route.”

**Why it is wrong:** Connectedness is not sameness, an uninterrupted route, or a substitute for proving the particular relation under discussion.
